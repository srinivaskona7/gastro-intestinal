## What it is

**Hepatitis** means inflammation of the liver. It can be viral, toxic (alcohol, drugs), autoimmune or metabolic. The important unifying idea is the natural history: persistent inflammation makes scar tissue, scar tissue distorts the liver into nodules (**cirrhosis**), and a cirrhotic liver is at risk of **hepatocellular cancer**. Hepatitis A and E are the exceptions because they cause only acute illness and do not become chronic.

This page covers the two commonest non-viral forms. Viral types are on the viral hepatitis page.

## Alcoholic hepatitis

### Causes and risk factors

Risk comes from "high risk" drinking, defined here as more than 50 standard drinks a week in men and more than 35 in women.

### Pathogenesis

Ethanol is metabolised in the liver to **acetaldehyde**, which is directly toxic to hepatocytes. It provokes severe inflammation, and repeated injury leads to **fibrosis**.

### Clinical features

| Finding | Explanation or note |
|---|---|
| Jaundice | Failing bilirubin handling and cholestasis |
| Hepatomegaly | Fatty liver |
| Splenomegaly | Only if portal hypertension has developed |
| Dupuytren's contracture | Thickened palmar fascia, a classic alcohol-related sign |
| Hepatic flap | Asterixis, a sign of encephalopathy |
| Truncal ataxia | Cerebellar involvement |
| Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome | Thiamine deficiency in heavy drinkers |
| Delirium tremens | Alcohol withdrawal |

### Investigations

- **AST and ALT raised** (hepatocyte injury).
- **GGT raised out of proportion to ALP** (a raised GGT:ALP ratio points to alcohol rather than bile-duct obstruction).

### Management

- Stop alcohol and avoid hepatotoxic drugs.
- Give **thiamine plus vitamin B12** if Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome is present.
- Give **diazepam** for delirium tremens.
- Careful fluid management.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Thiamine before glucose">
In a malnourished heavy drinker, a glucose load can precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy. Replace thiamine first.
</Callout>

## Paracetamol-induced hepatitis

### Cause

Paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose.

### Pathogenesis

At normal doses paracetamol is safely conjugated by **glutathione**. In overdose the glutathione stores run out, so free paracetamol (via its toxic metabolite) accumulates and directly poisons hepatocytes. The result is **toxin-mediated centrilobular necrosis**, which is death of cells around the central vein (zone 3), where oxygen and metabolic activity favour toxic metabolite production.

### Clinical features and tests

- Features of acute hepatitis: jaundice, confusion and altered level of consciousness.
- **AST and ALT raised**, reflecting necrosis.
- **Bilirubin raised** (predominantly unconjugated in this setting).

### Outcomes

1. Acute hepatic failure, leading to death.
2. Spontaneous recovery.
3. Recovery after treatment with N-acetylcysteine.

### Management

**N-acetylcysteine** is a precursor of glutathione, so it restores the conjugating capacity. The dose is titrated against the amount of paracetamol taken, using the **treatment nomogram**.

<Callout kind="pearl">
Antidote logic: the problem is lost glutathione, so give its precursor. The earlier it is given, the better the liver is protected.
</Callout>
