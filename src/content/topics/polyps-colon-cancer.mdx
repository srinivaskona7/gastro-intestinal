## What it is

Colonic tumours are growths of the large bowel lining. **Polyps** are the commonest. Most are benign, but a minority, the **adenomas**, can turn malignant. Of all neoplastic colonic tumours, **98% are adenocarcinomas**. Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a disease of Western, older populations and is the second most common internal malignancy.

## Causes and risk factors

| Risk factor | Detail |
|---|---|
| Age | 90% of CRC is diagnosed after 50 years |
| Pre-existing polyps | Most are benign; adenomas can become malignant |
| Genetics and family history | HNPCC and FAP are best known and carry a very high lifetime risk of cancer |
| Inflammatory bowel disease | Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis |
| Diet | High fat, low fibre, low calcium, low folate |
| Smoking | Adds risk |

## Sporadic versus inherited cancer

| | Sporadic | Inherited |
|---|---|---|
| Share of CRC | Over 95% | Under 5% |
| Usual site | Left (descending, distal) colon | Right (ascending, proximal) colon |
| Driver | Toxin and carcinogen exposure over time | Germline mutation |
| Typical bleeding | Frank rectal bleeding | Occult bleeding and anaemia |

## Pathogenesis

Whether a mutation is inherited or acquired, the result is the same: the cell cycle becomes dysregulated, the epithelium becomes **dysplastic**, and eventually frank **carcinoma** develops.

## Types of polyp

About **90% of polyps are non-neoplastic** and have low malignant potential. About **10% are neoplastic** and have high malignant potential.

<Mermaid
  caption="Colonic polyp classification"
  chart={`flowchart TD
  P["Colonic polyps"] --> N["Non-neoplastic, about 90 percent"]
  P --> M["Neoplastic, about 10 percent"]
  N --> H["Hyperplastic"]
  N --> HA["Hamartomatous or juvenile"]
  N --> I["Inflammatory or pseudopolyps"]
  M --> AD["Adenomas"]
  AD --> T["Tubular"]
  AD --> TV["Tubulovillous"]
  AD --> V["Villous"]`}
/>

### Non-neoplastic polyps

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Hyperplastic** | Commonest non-neoplastic polyp; descending colon. Multiple, small, sessile (stalkless), nipple-like. Microscopically deeper crypts with extra goblet cells (more mucus) giving a saw-tooth outline, with no dysplasia. Benign. |
| **Hamartomatous / juvenile** | A hamartoma is a non-malignant overgrowth that grows at the same rate as surrounding tissue. Typical of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome (autosomal dominant). Large and irregular; dilated glands of normal cells in abnormal arrangement; no dysplasia. Benign, but can cause obstruction. |
| **Inflammatory / pseudopolyps** | Seen in inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis). |

### Adenomas (neoplastic)

| Type | Shape | Frequency among adenomas | Look |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tubular | Pedunculated (on a stalk) | About 90% | Smooth mushroom |
| Tubulovillous | Pedunculated | Under 10% | Mixed appearance |
| Villous | Sessile | Under 1% | Wart-like |

### Inherited syndromes

| | HNPCC (Lynch syndrome) | FAP |
|---|---|---|
| Share of CRC | Under 1% | About 1%, rare |
| Inheritance | Autosomal dominant | Autosomal dominant |
| Polyps | A few adenomatous polyps | Thousands of adenomatous polyps |
| Lifetime CRC risk | 70-80% | Essentially 100% |
| Other cancers | Endometrial, gastric, ovarian, small intestinal, ureter, renal pelvis | Same group of non-colonic cancers |

Most individual FAP polyps are benign, but there are far too many to remove, so cancer is effectively inevitable without colectomy.

**Amsterdam II criteria** support a diagnosis of HNPCC when:

1. Three or more relatives have an HNPCC-associated cancer.
2. At least one is a first-degree relative of the other two.
3. The family history spans two or more generations.
4. At least one case was diagnosed before age 50.

<Callout kind="pearl">
Right-sided cancers bleed silently, so the patient presents with iron-deficiency anaemia. Left-sided cancers have a narrower lumen and bleed visibly, with altered bowel habit.
</Callout>

## Clinical features

- Often **asymptomatic** early on.
- Alternating bowel habit, and change in stool shape (thin, ribbon-like stool).
- Cramping, colicky pain, discomfort and bloating.
- Rectal bleeding: frank on the left, occult with anaemia on the right. Occult blood is detected by a **faecal occult blood test (FOBT)**.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Red flags">
Anorexia, fever, night sweats, weight loss, fatigue and anaemia suggest advanced or bleeding disease and need urgent investigation.
</Callout>

## Investigations

- **FOBT** for screening and occult bleeding.
- **Colonoscopy** to see the lesion, remove polyps (polypectomy) and take biopsies.
- **Imaging** (CT, PET; a barium enema is the barium study for the colon, a barium swallow examines the upper tract).
- **Genetic testing** if there is a family history.
- **FBC** to detect anaemia.

## Management

<Mermaid
  caption="Pathway from detection to treatment"
  chart={`flowchart LR
  A["FOBT or symptoms"] --> B["Colonoscopy with biopsy"]
  B --> C["Grading"]
  C --> D["CT, PET or MRI staging (TNM or Dukes A to D)"]
  D --> E["Bowel resection and possible colostomy"]
  E --> F["Adjuvant options"]
  F --> G["Targeted therapy, chemotherapy, radiotherapy"]`}
/>

- Polypectomy or biopsy gives the histological grade.
- CT, PET or MRI stage the tumour (TNM, or Dukes A to D).
- **Bowel resection**, with a colostomy where needed.
- **Targeted therapy** with cetuximab or panitumumab, antibodies against the epidermal growth factor receptor.
- Chemotherapy and radiotherapy in selected cases.

## Complications

- Perforation, peritonitis, abscess or fistula.
- Metastasis, which leads to death.
