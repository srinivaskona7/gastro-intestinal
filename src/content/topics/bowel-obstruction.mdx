## What it is

A **bowel obstruction** is a mechanical blockage of the intestinal lumen. Fluid, gas and food collect above the block, the bowel dilates, and nothing passes below it. **Obstipation** means absolute constipation: no faeces and no flatus.

The usual causes are:

| Site of problem | Examples |
|---|---|
| Outside the bowel | Adhesions from previous surgery, hernias, volvulus |
| In the wall | Tumours, Crohn's strictures, diverticular strictures |
| In the lumen | Faecal impaction, gallstone ileus, foreign bodies, intussusception |

## Clinical features

Every obstruction produces the same general picture:

- Abdominal distension
- Nausea and vomiting
- Crampy (colicky) abdominal pain
- Possibly obstipation

The pattern then depends on **where** the block is and whether the blood supply is threatened.

### Non-strangulating obstruction

| Level of block | Typical features |
|---|---|
| Proximal | Profuse vomiting, often bilious; little distension |
| Middle | Moderate vomiting, distension, obstipation |
| Distal | Late **faeculent** vomiting, marked distension, obstipation |

The logic is simple: a high block lets almost nothing through to the stomach to be retained, so vomiting dominates. A low block leaves a long length of bowel to fill, so distension is large and vomiting comes late.

### Strangulating obstruction

Here the bowel wall loses its blood supply (reduced perfusion), which leads to **necrosis**. Look for:

- Early shock
- Fever, tachycardia and leucocytosis
- Vomit containing gross or occult blood
- Peritonitis
- Possibly melaena

<Callout kind="warning" title="Strangulation is a surgical emergency">
Fever, tachycardia, a raised white count, blood in vomit or signs of peritonitis in someone with obstruction mean the bowel may be dying. Delay increases mortality sharply.
</Callout>

## Investigations

**Imaging**

- **Upright chest X-ray** looks for free air under the diaphragm (perforation).
- **Abdominal X-ray** shows dilated loops of bowel and air-fluid levels.
- **CT** is the adjunct that gives the definitive diagnosis, locates the cause and judges severity.
- **Ultrasound** can be useful in pregnant patients, avoiding radiation.

**Laboratory**

- Chemistry may show dehydration.
- Leucocytosis, raised serum lactate and LDH, and raised amylase.
- **Metabolic alkalosis** if a proximal small bowel obstruction causes heavy vomiting (loss of gastric acid).
- **Metabolic acidosis** if bowel has infarcted (lactate release).

## Management

1. **Nasogastric tube** to decompress the stomach and relieve vomiting and distension.
2. **Resuscitate**: intravenous fluids and electrolytes to restore haemodynamics.
3. **Urinary catheter** to monitor fluid balance.
4. **Surgical referral**.
5. Partial small bowel obstruction is generally treated **conservatively**; complete small bowel obstruction goes to **surgery**.

## Prognosis and complications

| Type | Mortality |
|---|---|
| Non-strangulating | 2% |
| Strangulating | 8% (25% if more than 36 hours) |
| Strangulating and ischaemic | 85% |

Complications follow the strangulation pathway: necrosis, then **bowel perforation**, **sepsis** and **hypovolaemia or shock**.

<Callout kind="pearl">
Vomiting early and heavy means high block; distension large and vomiting faeculent and late means low block. Alkalosis from vomiting is expected, but acidosis suggests infarcted bowel.
</Callout>
