## Why the gut secretes

Secretions fall into three groups, each with its own purpose.

| Component | Roles |
|---|---|
| Proteins | Digestive enzymes; mucin for protection and lubrication; immunoglobulins in saliva as antimicrobials; peptide hormones |
| Ions | Hold pH steady; keep luminal osmolality near 300 mOsm; build electrochemical gradients that drive ion movement across cells |
| Water | Solvent and transport medium; needed for chemical reactions; keeps osmolality right; gives food and waste a consistency suitable for ingestion, digestion and excretion |

## Volumes and contents

| Source | Volume per day | Key contents |
|---|---|---|
| Pancreas | About 1500 mL | Bicarbonate (neutralises acid); amylase, lipase, proteases, nucleases |
| Intestines | About 1000 mL | Slightly alkaline intestinal juice; in the duodenum, Brunner's glands add bicarbonate and mucus |
| Colon | Negligible | Mucus |

Adding the 2000 mL of drinking water, total fluid handled is about 9000 mL a day. The remainder comes from saliva, gastric juice and bile, with textbook figures of roughly 1500, 2500 and 500 mL.

## Secretory structures

### Salivary glands

| Gland | Secretes |
|---|---|
| Parotid | Alpha-amylase (splits amylose, straight-chain starch) and antimicrobial agents |
| Submandibular | Mucus and alpha-amylase |
| Sublingual | Mucus |

### Stomach

The surface mucosa is made entirely of goblet-type cells that produce thick mucus and **bicarbonate**. The bicarbonate neutralises H+ in the mucus gel (the unstirred layer), protecting the wall from its own acid.

- **Parietal cells** lie in the middle of the gland among the chief cells. **Intrinsic factor** binds ingested vitamin B12 so that it can be absorbed.
- **Chief cells** sit at the base. Pepsinogen is the inactive form of the protease pepsin; **acid converts pepsinogen to pepsin**.
- **Enteroendocrine cells** release messengers into the surrounding tissue.

### Duodenum, liver and pancreas

- **Brunner's glands** are compound tubular glands in the duodenum above the hepatopancreatic sphincter. Their alkaline fluid and mucus help neutralise acidic chyme.
- **Liver:** makes bile (bile salts, phospholipids and emulsifiers) for fat digestion and absorption.
- **Exocrine pancreas:** bicarbonate; amylase (starch to maltose and glucose); lipase (fats to fatty acids and monoglycerides); proteases; nucleases (DNA and RNA to nucleotides).

### Intestinal crypts of Lieberkuhn

| Cell | Job |
|---|---|
| Paneth cells | Defensins and lysozyme, for intestinal immune defence |
| Stem cells | At the crypt base; divide rapidly, replace wall cells, migrate up the villi and are shed within days |
| Epithelial cells | Slightly alkaline intestinal juice |
| Goblet cells | Mucus |

## How secretion works

### Exportable proteins

1. A ribosome translates mRNA and docks on the rough ER through the signal-recognition particle, delivering the protein into the ER.
2. Vesicles bud from the rough ER and travel to the Golgi.
3. The Golgi modifies the protein and sends vesicles to the apical surface.
4. Contents are released by exocytosis, or fuse with the membrane. Mucin from goblet cells is the example.

### Ions and water

Every ion movement is either active transport, or passive movement down a concentration or electrical gradient. **Na+/K+ ATPase** uses ATP to build those gradients, which secondary transporters then exploit. Ion movement draws water by osmosis through **aquaporins**.

## Worked examples

### Fluid secretion by salivary glands and pancreatic acini

The aim is to bring water into the lumen, since saliva and pancreatic juice are mostly water carrying enzymes, ions, bicarbonate and antibodies.

1. The Na+/K+/2Cl- cotransporter imports chloride using the sodium gradient created by Na+/K+ ATPase.
2. Chloride is secreted into the lumen, with sodium following to balance charge.
3. Rising luminal ion concentration pulls water in through aquaporins.
4. Further along the duct, ions are actively reabsorbed but water is not.

### Bicarbonate secretion by pancreatic ducts, Brunner's glands and bile ducts

The result is an alkaline fluid that neutralises acid in pancreatic juice, the duodenum and bile. The tools are the Na+/H+ antiporter (driven by the sodium gradient), carbonic anhydrase, Na+/K+ ATPase and ion channels.

### Gastric acid secretion by parietal cells

- Three stimulants act: **acetylcholine**, **gastrin** and **histamine**.
- Acetylcholine and gastrin raise intracellular calcium, needed for protein kinase activity.
- Histamine raises cAMP, which activates protein kinase.
- Protein kinase activates the **H+/K+ ATPase**, which pumps H+ into the lumen.
- Carbonic anhydrase supplies the H+ from CO2 + H2O. The leftover bicarbonate leaves through a HCO3-/Cl- antiporter, which brings chloride in for secretion with the acid.

| Regulator | Type | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Acetylcholine | Neurotransmitter | Enteric neurons |
| Histamine | Paracrine | ECL (enterochromaffin-like) cells |
| Gastrin | Hormone | G cells of gastric epithelium |
| Somatostatin | Paracrine and hormone, the inhibitor | D cells of gastric epithelium |

### Drugs and other agents

| Agent | Effect on acid |
|---|---|
| Omeprazole | Proton-pump inhibitor; blocks the final step of H+ secretion, the most efficient and effective |
| Ranitidine | H2-receptor antagonist; no cAMP, so acid production slows |
| Caffeine | Blocks phosphodiesterase, so cAMP persists and acid rises |
| Pentagastrin | Synthetic gastrin-like hormone; increases gastric secretion |

<Callout kind="pearl">
The final common step is the H+/K+ ATPase. That is why a proton-pump inhibitor beats a drug that blocks only one of the three stimulants.
</Callout>
