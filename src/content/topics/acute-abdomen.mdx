## Why it matters

Abdominal pain is a very common reason to attend the Emergency Department. The key task is to separate patients who need an **urgent operation (laparotomy)** from those who do not.

## Laparotomy or not?

| Needs laparotomy | Features |
|---|---|
| Organ rupture (spleen, aorta, ectopic pregnancy) | Shock |
| Generalised peritonitis from perforation (peptic or duodenal ulcer, diverticulum, appendix, bowel, gallbladder) | Prostration, shock, lying still, tenderness with guarding, rebound and percussion pain, abdominal rigidity, absent bowel sounds |

| Does not need laparotomy | Features |
|---|---|
| Local peritonitis (diverticulitis, cholecystitis, salpingitis, appendicitis) | Lying still, tenderness (guarding, rebound, percussion), rigidity |
| Colic | Restlessness; regularly waxing and waning pain |

## Tests and priorities

- **Bloods:** U&E, FBC, amylase, LFT, CRP and arterial blood gas; plus urinalysis.
- **Erect chest X-ray** to find **air under the diaphragm**, a sign of perforation.
- **Resuscitate before surgery.** Anaesthesia compounds shock, so an unresuscitated patient may collapse on induction.

## Three types of abdominal pain

| Feature | Visceral (colicky) | Somatic (parietal) | Referred |
|---|---|---|---|
| Source | Abdominal viscera, usually from pressure or stretch | Inflamed or irritated parietal peritoneum | Pathology elsewhere, felt at another site |
| Nerves | Autonomic fibres | Somatic nerves | Shared embryonic dermatomes |
| Localisation | Diffuse, poorly localised, often periumbilical | Very well localised | Distant site |
| Quality | Fluctuating, comes and goes | Sharp; worse on moving, coughing, breathing | Varies |
| Associations | Nausea and autonomic features such as sweating | Peritonism | Diaphragm irritation gives shoulder-tip pain |
| Behaviour | Moves around, cannot get comfortable | Keeps still | |

### Extra-abdominal causes to remember

- **Inferior myocardial infarction** often presents with epigastric pain.
- **Testicular torsion** can present with hypogastric pain.

## Assessing the pain

Ask whether it is poorly defined or well localised, and **what the pain makes the patient do**.

- **Waxing and waning** pain suggests obstruction of something (a tube).
- **Constant** pain: sharp suggests peritoneal involvement, dull suggests visceral.
- **Aggravating or relieving factors:** better or worse on eating (worse with food in gastric or duodenal ulcer disease), and worse on movement.
- **Progression:** pathology often moves from visceral to parietal pain. In appendicitis the pain starts as diffuse visceral pain, then irritates the peritoneum and becomes localised somatic pain.

Pain behaviour differs: **colic** (biliary, renal) makes patients move about; **parietal pain** (for example from bleeding or infection) makes them keep still.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Silent, rigid, shocked">
A still, shocked patient with a rigid abdomen and no bowel sounds has peritonitis until proven otherwise. Resuscitate, then operate.
</Callout>
