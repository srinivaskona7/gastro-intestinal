## Background: protection versus destruction

The stomach lining survives a pH of about 2 because of defences that balance destructive forces.

| Protective factors | Destructive factors |
|---|---|
| Alkaline mucus layer, a mechanical barrier | **Helicobacter pylori** |
| Prostaglandins, which stimulate mucin synthesis by goblet cells | Acid (pH about 2) |
| | Pepsin, a proteolytic enzyme from chief cells |
| | NSAIDs (aspirin, ibuprofen): 15-20% of users develop a gastric ulcer |
| | Stress and gastrinoma (Zollinger-Ellison syndrome) |

## Gastritis

**Gastritis** is inflammation of the stomach lining.

### Causes and pathogenesis

| Type | Cause and mechanism |
|---|---|
| Acute | Alcohol (about 15%); NSAIDs inhibit COX, so prostaglandins fall, giving hyperacidity and inflammation; severe burns reduce plasma volume and the mucosa sloughs |
| Chronic | About 80% bacterial, mainly Helicobacter pylori, the most common cause |
| Atrophic | Autoimmune: antibodies against parietal cells and intrinsic factor cause pernicious anaemia (vitamin B12 deficiency) |

### Clinical features

Abdominal pain, dyspepsia, bloating, nausea and vomiting. There may be haematemesis if an ulcer is present, and anaemia if pernicious B12 deficiency exists.

### Investigations and management

- **Carbon-13 urea breath test**, serology (IgG) and **faecal antigen test** for H. pylori.
- **Endoscopy with gastric biopsy**: H. pylori microscopy and exclusion of gastric cancer.

Treatment: avoid triggers (alcohol, NSAIDs); antacids (Mylanta); PPI (omeprazole) or H2 antagonist (ranitidine); **H. pylori triple eradication therapy** (clarithromycin plus amoxicillin, with or without metronidazole); B12 injections for pernicious anaemia.

## Peptic ulcer disease

### Causes

Ulcers result from **too much attack** (hyperacidity, Zollinger-Ellison syndrome) or **too little defence** (H. pylori, stress, drugs such as NSAIDs and corticosteroids, smoking).

### Morphology

A small, single, round, **punched-out** ulcer. About 90% lie in the **duodenum or the lesser curve** of the stomach. Healing ulcers show mucosal folds radiating from the scar as it contracts.

### Clinical features

- Burning epigastric pain, worst when hungry and relieved by food
- Nausea and vomiting
- Anorexia and weight loss
- Haematemesis or melaena; perforation leads to acute peritonitis

### Investigations

Clinical history; endoscopy with biopsy (ulcer, H. pylori, cancer); **carbon-13 urea breath test**, the best non-invasive H. pylori diagnosis; serology (IgG); faecal antigen test.

### Management

Same medical regimen as gastritis: avoid triggers, antacids, PPI or H2 antagonist, and triple eradication therapy. **Emergency surgery** is needed for haematemesis, rupture, peritonitis or cancer.

### Complications

| Complication | Consequence |
|---|---|
| GI bleeding | Anaemia |
| Perforation | Haemorrhage and shock, peritonitis, or erosion into the pancreas (pancreatitis) |
| Pyloric stenosis from scarring | Gastric outlet obstruction and vomiting |
| Gastric cancer | H. pylori carries about six times the cancer risk |

<Callout kind="warning" title="Perforation">
Sudden severe abdominal pain with peritonism in an ulcer patient means perforation: an emergency.
</Callout>

## Curling's ulcer

An acute peptic ulcer (of the duodenum) caused by severe burns. Burns cause fluid loss and a fall in plasma volume, the gastric mucosa sloughs, and an ulcer forms. Risk of perforation and haemorrhage is high, with high mortality.

**Prevention**: total parenteral nutrition and a PPI or H2 receptor antagonist; emergency surgery if haematemesis, rupture or peritonitis.

<Callout kind="pearl">
Hunger pain relieved by food points to a duodenal ulcer. Always test for H. pylori and biopsy gastric ulcers to exclude cancer.
</Callout>
