## What it is

Five main viruses (A to E) infect hepatocytes. They differ in how they spread and whether they persist.

| Virus | Course | Spread | Cirrhosis risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Acute only | Faecal-oral | None |
| B | Acute and chronic | Blood, sex, mother to baby | Yes |
| C | Mostly chronic | Blood, body fluids | Yes |
| D | Acute and chronic, needs B | With hepatitis B | Yes, faster |
| E | Acute only | Faecal-oral | None |

## Hepatitis A

**Hepatitis A virus** is the commonest viral hepatitis worldwide. It spreads faecal-orally, with an incubation period of 2 to 6 weeks. The virus damages the liver directly (cytopathic) but never causes cirrhosis, and there is no chronic phase.

**Clinical features.** Viraemia gives fever, malaise, anorexia, nausea and arthralgia. After 1 to 2 weeks jaundice appears because of intrahepatic cholestasis: conjugated bilirubin rises, giving **pale stools and dark urine**. There may be hepatomegaly, splenomegaly and tender lymph nodes. Hepatic encephalopathy and death are rare.

**Investigations.** All liver tests are raised; confirm with hepatitis A serology and PCR.

**Prognosis.** Usually self-limiting; supportive care only.

## Hepatitis E

Clinically it mimics hepatitis A: faecal-oral spread (including animal sources such as dogs, pigs and rodents), direct liver cell damage, raised liver tests. The key difference is danger in **pregnancy**.

- Mortality is 1 to 2% overall, from fulminant hepatic failure.
- In pregnancy mortality is about **20%**, from DIC in the third trimester.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Common confusions">
Hepatitis E is not a herpesvirus; it is an RNA virus in its own family. Diagnosis needs hepatitis E serology and PCR, not the hepatitis A tests.
</Callout>

## Hepatitis B

### Transmission

- **Horizontal**: sexual contact, blood, ingestion and inoculation. Highly infectious.
- **Vertical**: mother to baby, with a 20% chance. Infected mothers need anti-HBV immunoglobulin plus oral antivirals.
- Preventable by vaccination with surface antigen, given at birth, 2, 4 and 6 months and at year 8.

### Pathogenesis

Hepatocyte injury is caused by **CD8 T-cell attack**, not the virus directly. An immunocompetent person clears the virus and recovers. If the immune response is sub-optimal the infection persists in one of two ways:

### Clinical picture by stage

| | Acute | Chronic active (30%) | Chronic persistent (70%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Symptoms | Fever, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, myalgia | Same non-specific viral symptoms | May be asymptomatic |
| Signs | Conjugated jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, pruritus | Jaundice, small nodular liver, portal hypertension signs (telangiectasia, caput medusae, ascites, pedal oedema, splenomegaly, gynaecomastia, encephalopathy) | None |
| Nature | Acute illness | Active, replicative | Inactive carrier |
| Outcome | Over 90% full recovery | Cirrhosis in all; liver cancer risk about 200 times higher | Carrier for life, minimal progression, late cirrhosis, sporadic acute episodes |

### Serology

| State | HBsAg | Anti-HBs | HBeAg | Anti-HBe | Anti-HBc | LFTs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Acute | Positive | Negative | Positive | Negative | Positive | AST, ALT, bilirubin raised |
| Chronic active | Positive | Negative | Positive | Negative | Positive | Raised |
| Chronic persistent | Positive | Negative | Negative | Positive | Positive | Normal |
| Recovered | Negative | Positive | Negative | Positive | Positive | Normal |
| Vaccinated | Negative | Positive | Negative | Negative | Negative | Normal |

<Callout kind="pearl">
Anti-HBs alone means vaccination. Anti-HBs plus anti-HBc means a past infection. Recovery is anti-HBs positive with HBsAg cleared.
</Callout>

Treatment: interferon alpha and long-term antivirals, with remission only about 20 to 40%, plus maternal prophylaxis. (Standard hepatitis B antivirals are nucleoside analogues such as tenofovir or entecavir, not ganciclovir.)

## Hepatitis D

The hepatitis D virus cannot replicate alone and needs hepatitis B, so it appears as **co-infection**. Injury is immune mediated, as in B, but more severe, with quicker progression to cirrhosis. Symptoms are indistinguishable from acute hepatitis B.

- **Diagnosis**: raised AST/ALT, IgM anti-HDV and IgM anti-HBc.
- **Complications**: higher risk of fulminant hepatitis, and chronic hepatitis D carries about 70% risk of cirrhosis, more than B alone.

## Hepatitis C

**Transmission**: blood (for example injecting drug use; as little as 0.0001 mL can transmit), other body fluids including semen and cervical secretions, and rarely vertical.

**Pathogenesis**: the virus is not directly cytopathic. Damage is from the immune response, giving chronic low-grade inflammation, then fibrosis and cirrhosis.

**Natural history**

- About 10% have an acute illness (mild viral illness with jaundice) and recover.
- About 90% become chronic: asymptomatic for years, usually found by chance, with sporadic mild illnesses and jaundice.
- End stage: 20 to 30% develop cirrhosis within 10 to 30 years; about 5% develop hepatocellular carcinoma (the virus inactivates p53).

**Investigations**: mildly raised ALT/AST on routine tests, anti-HCV serology, HCV-RNA by PCR.

**Treatment**: after exposure such as a needlestick, interferon and ribavirin; established disease gets supportive care.

## Other infections affecting the liver

| Agent | Liver findings | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| EBV (glandular fever) | Acute hepatitic changes within 5 days (degeneration, swelling, necrosis, inflammation); periportal large mononuclear cells | Mild jaundice and mildly raised AST/ALT common; clinical hepatitis uncommon |
| CMV | Biopsy shows intranuclear inclusions and giant cells | Especially in the immunocompromised |
| Yellow fever virus | Acute hepatic necrosis, significant | Jaundice, markedly raised AST/ALT |
| Herpes simplex | Massive necrosis on biopsy | Immunocompromised or pregnant; markedly raised AST/ALT; treat with aciclovir |
| Toxoplasmosis | Picture like EBV | Mild jaundice, mildly raised AST/ALT |
